\chapter{Introduction}
\label{Chapter::Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

In \gls{NMPC}, a nonlinear \gls{OCP} is solved in each sampling period 
to compute the optimal control input for the current state.
To solve the continuous-time \glspl{OCP}, 
direct multiple shooting~\cite{bock1984multiple} is a widely used method,
which transcribes the \gls{OCP} into a \gls{NLP} by discretizing the time horizon into multiple shooting intervals~\cite{Diehl2018}.
\Gls{SQP}~\cite{nocedal2006numerical} provides a suitable approach for solving the \gls{NLP},
which linearizes to obtain a \gls{QP} subproblem and solves it to obtain the search direction at each iterate.
In the real-time iteration~\cite{diehl2005real},
one \gls{SQP} iteration is performed per sampling time, with the linearization done before the new state is known,
so only the \gls{QP} solve lies between measurement and feedback.
The \gls{QP} solve therefore requires efficiency, for which structure-exploiting solvers are usually used.

When the exact Hessian of the Lagrangian is used in \gls{QP} subproblems~\cite{BoggsTolle1995},
the full Hessian of \gls{QP} subproblems could be indefinite.
The \gls{QP} solvers embedded in the \gls{SQP} for exploiting the \gls{OCP} structure,
such as qpDUNES~\cite{frasch2015parallel}, FORCES~\cite{domahidi2012efficient} and HPIPM~\cite{frison2020hpipmhighperformancequadraticprogramming}, 
however, may fail to solve these \glspl{QP}, for they require convex \glspl{QP} with a positive definite full Hessian.
One method to use these solvers in this situation is to regularize the full Hessian
at the \gls{SQP} level before it is passed to the solver, while preserving the \gls{OCP} structure.
Two common regularization methods mentioned in~\cite{verschueren2017sparsity}, i.e.\ project and mirror,
are implemented in acados~\cite{verschueren2020acadosmodularopensourceframework} 
and used to yield a positive definite full Hessian for the \gls{QP} solver.
Both methods regularize based on the eigenvalues of the full Hessian and use a small positive value as a threshold.
Project raises every eigenvalue to at least the threshold,
while mirror replaces the negative eigenvalues with their absolute values, using the threshold as a lower bound.
This, however, in general modifies the solution of the subproblems and also the feedback itself in the real-time iteration.

Convexification~\cite{verschueren2017sparsity}, as a more advanced regularization method,
applies a backward sweep similar to Riccati recursion for regularization.
The sweep transfers cost between neighboring stages through the dynamics 
and adds curvature from the estimated active inequality constraints with an estimated weight to each stage.
The cost transfer keeps only the needed curvature for each stage to be positive definite 
while leaving the reduced Hessian, i.e.\ the Hessian on the null space of the dynamics, unchanged.
The added curvature makes the reduced Hessian positive definite 
if the estimation of the active constraints and weight is correct and
the fully reduced Hessian, i.e.\ the Hessian on the null space of the dynamics and the active constraints, is positive definite.
This results in a positive definite full Hessian with the fully reduced Hessian unchanged,
and the same primal solution of the original \gls{QP} is thus kept.
But if the fully reduced Hessian is indefinite or the estimation is incorrect, 
the backward sweep could still reach a stage with indefinite blocks,
and convexification falls back to projecting the stage block, which modifies the \gls{QP} solution.
In acados, only the cost transfer in convexification is implemented, 
so it could fail to regularize with inequality constraints in practice.

    \caption{Regularization overview of an indefinite \gls{QP} subproblem}

In an interior-point \gls{QP} solver, however,
the \gls{IPM} relaxes the complementarity from each inequality constraint by a barrier parameter.
This adds weighted curvatures from inequality constraints to the Hessian inherently throughout the iterations.
The weight changes according to the current iterate, 
so neither an estimate of the active set nor a penalty weight is needed.
The reduced Hessian with curvature from inequality constraints would be positive definite,
as long as the fully reduced Hessian is positive definite.

\begin{example}[A \gls{QP} with an indefinite reduced Hessian]
\label{ex:toy}
Consider an indefinite \gls{QP}
\begin{mini}|l|
  {x_0,u_0,x_1,u_1,x_2}
  {\tfrac{1}{2}(u_0^2 - 3x_1^2 + u_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}x_2^2)}
  {\label{eq:toy_qp}}
  {}
  \addConstraint{x_0}{=1}
  \addConstraint{x_{1}}{=x_0 + u_0}
  \addConstraint{x_{2}}{=x_1 + u_1}
  \addConstraint{-1}{\le u_0 \le 1.}{}
\end{mini}
By eliminating $x_0$, $x_1$ and $x_2$ using the equality constraints,
we have a \gls{QP} with an indefinite reduced Hessian as:
\begin{mini}|l|
  {u_0,u_1}
  {\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{3}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}
   + \begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{5}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}}
  {\label{eq:toy_qp_reduced}}
  {}
  \addConstraint{-1}{\le u_0 \le 1.}{}
\end{mini}

Denote the objective of~\eqref{eq:toy_qp_reduced} by $f(u_0,u_1)$.
For a fixed $u_0$, the minimizer of the objective over $u_1$ follows from
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial u_1} = \tfrac{1}{2}u_0 + \tfrac{3}{2}u_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  u_1^\star(u_0) = -\tfrac{1}{3}(u_0+1).
\end{equation*}
Substituting $u_1^\star(u_0)$ back into $f$ leaves a function of $u_0$ alone,
\begin{equation*}
  f\bigl(u_0,u_1^\star\bigr) = -\tfrac{5}{6}u_0^2 - \tfrac{8}{3}u_0 - \tfrac{1}{12}.
\end{equation*}
Since $f\bigl(u_0,u_1^\star\bigr)$ is concave, its minimum over $-1 \le u_0 \le 1$ lies on a bound,
and comparing $f\bigl(-1, 0\bigr) = \tfrac{7}{4}$ with $f\bigl(1,-\frac{2}{3}\bigr)= -\tfrac{43}{12}$ gives
the exact solution $(1, -\frac{2}{3})$.

The contour lines of the reduced objective are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_sqp}a
with a saddle point in reduced space and the exact solution lying at $(1, -\frac{2}{3})$ on the constraint boundary.

From an \gls{SQP} perspective, 
this \gls{QP} can occur either as a subproblem that results in an \gls{SQP} step or as an \gls{NLP} that results in a solution.
In the former case, the \gls{SQP}-level regularization, here mirror, modifies the \gls{QP} before it is passed to the \gls{QP} solver.
The resulting solution, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_sqp}b, is approximate for the \gls{QP} subproblem
and also an approximate Newton step for \gls{SQP}.
For completeness, Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_sqp}c shows the latter case,
where \gls{SQP} takes this approximate step first and needs several iterations to reach the exact solution.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{images/introduction/toy_bound_sqp_level.pdf}
    \caption{
    Contour lines of a \gls{QP} with an indefinite reduced Hessian.
    (a) shows the original objective, 
    (b) shows the objective of the \gls{QP} after mirror if it is treated as a subproblem,
    and (c) shows the \gls{SQP} iterates when the \gls{QP} is solved as an \gls{NLP} for completeness.}
    \label{fig:toy_example_sqp}
\end{figure}

From a \gls{QP} perspective,
we can exploit the curvature contributions from inequality constraints in the \gls{IPM}.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_ipm}b, 
the curvature contributions from inequality constraints can make the reduced Hessian positive definite,
and the local second-order model can thus produce a direction towards the exact solution without any extra work.
The \gls{IPM} can thus solve the indefinite \gls{QP} in several \gls{QP} iterations, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_ipm}c.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{images/introduction/toy_bound_ipm_level.pdf}
    \caption{
    Contour lines of a \gls{QP} with an indefinite reduced Hessian.
    (a) shows the original objective, 
    (b) shows the local second-order model with curvature contributions from inequality constraints,
    and (c) shows the \gls{IPM} iterates on the \gls{QP}.}
    \label{fig:toy_example_ipm}
\end{figure}

\end{example}

In other cases, such as in the early \gls{SQP} iterations,
where the curvature contribution may not be enough to make the reduced Hessian positive definite, 
a regularization to the Hessian is still needed.
But if the regularization is made inside the solver, 
the solution of the original \gls{QP} remains, and thus an exact Newton step in \gls{SQP} is preserved.

A structure-exploiting interior-point \gls{QP} solver that handles indefiniteness at the \gls{QP} level is 
therefore interesting as an alternative to regularizing at the \gls{SQP} level.
And this leaves open how the regularization should be made inside the solver.
This thesis addresses this question for indefinite \glspl{QP} with \gls{OCP} structure.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
The main contributions of this thesis are the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Riccati-based regularization}: 
    This thesis investigates regularizations inside the interior-point \gls{QP} solvers.
    The main focus is the proposed Riccati-based regularization,
    which exploits the \gls{OCP} structure of the problem, and regularizes the Hessian only when Cholesky factorization fails.
    This thesis further analyzes why the minimum perturbation in this regularization fails,
    improves the regularization strategy,
    and proves the regularization to be inactive near the solution (Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Primal_Regularization}).

    \item \textbf{A benchmark of $175$ indefinite \gls{OCP} \glspl{QP}}:
    This thesis provides a pipeline for collecting \glspl{QP} from the \gls{SQP} iterations with acados,
    and collects $175$ \glspl{QP} from $8$ nonlinear \glspl{OCP},
    together with an analysis of the indefiniteness regarding the benchmark (Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Benchmark}).

    \item \textbf{Numerical comparison and ablation}:
    This thesis implements a prototype solver that shows identical iteration behavior to HPIPM on convex \glspl{QP}, 
    and performs experiments with this solver on the benchmark to investigate the performance of the methods. 
    In addition, this thesis presents an analysis of solving failures 
    and includes ablation studies on the solver settings to show which components the results depend on~(Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Outline}
\label{sec:outline}
The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.
In Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Theoretical_Background}, 
we start with theoretical background regarding the primal--dual \gls{IPM} for \gls{OCP}-structured \glspl{QP}, 
where the Riccati recursion is used to solve the \glspl{QP}, 
and introduce the barrier update and step size control strategies adopted in this work.
In Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Primal_Regularization},
we propose the Riccati-based primal regularization,
derive the improved stage regularization strategy from the failure of minimum perturbation,
and prove that the regularization is asymptotically inactive.
Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Benchmark} presents 
the benchmark of indefinite \glspl{QP} constructed from the \gls{SQP} iterations of eight \glspl{OCP},
and analyzes how the indefiniteness is distributed over the stages and where it originates.
Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Experiments} first describes the prototype solver, the experiment setup,
and the numerical results.
The experiments compare the Riccati-based regularizations with full Hessian regularization and inertia correction on the benchmark,
and discuss the instances on which the methods fail, with ablations for investigating which components the results depend on.
Finally, Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Conclusion} summarizes the results and outlines directions for future work.
