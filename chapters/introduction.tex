\chapter{Introduction}
\label{Chapter::Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

In \gls{NMPC}, a nonlinear \gls{OCP} is solved in each sampling period 
to compute the optimal control input for the current state.
To solve the continuous-time \glspl{OCP}, 
direct multiple shooting~\cite{bock1984multiple} is a widely used method,
which transcribes the \gls{OCP} into a \gls{NLP} by discretizing the time horizon into multiple shooting intervals~\cite{Diehl2018}.
\Gls{SQP}~\cite{nocedal2006numerical} provides a suitable approach for solving the \gls{NLP} in real time,
which linearizes at each iterate to obtain a \gls{QP} subproblem.
The real-time iteration~\cite{diehl2005real} performs one \gls{SQP} iteration per sampling time and linearizes before the new state is known,
so only the \gls{QP} solve lies between measurement and feedback.
The \gls{QP} solve therefore require efficiency, for which the structure-exploiting solvers are usually used.

Nevertheless, when the full Hessian of the \gls{QP} subproblems is provided 
by the exact Hessian of the Lagrangian~\cite{BoggsTolle1995}, which could be indefinite.
The structure-exploiting solvers embedded in the \gls{SQP},
such as qpDUNES~\cite{frasch2015parallel}, FORCES~\cite{domahidi2012efficient} and HPIPM~\cite{frison2020hpipmhighperformancequadraticprogramming}, 
however, fail to solve these \glspl{QP}, for they require convex \glspl{QP} with a \gls{PD} full Hessian.
Though FORCES and HPIPM can in principle handle the nonconvex \glspl{QP} with an indefinite full Hessian as long as the reduced Hessian,
i.e.\ the Hessian on the null space of the dynamics, is \gls{PD},
the code optimization of both solvers still requires a \gls{PD} full Hessian and thus targets convex \glspl{QP}.
One method to use these solvers in this situation is to regularize at the \gls{SQP} level, 
i.e.\ to regularize the full Hessian of the \gls{QP} before it is passed to the solver,
which in general modifies the solution of the subproblems and in the real-time iteration the feedback itself.

Given that the augmented reduced Hessian, 
i.e.\ the Hessian on the null space of the dynamics and the active constraints, is \gls{PD},
CONVEXIFY~\cite{verschueren2017sparsity} can avoid the modification of the primal solution 
when the full Hessian is not \gls{PD}.
It applies a backward sweep similar to Riccati recursion to transfer cost between neighboring stages,
and thus each stage keeps only the curvature it needs to be \gls{PD}, so the reduced Hessian remains invariant during this transfer.
It also adds curvature by a quadratic penalty on the estimated active constraints with a tunable weight to make the reduced Hessian \gls{PD}.
Together, the full Hessian becomes \gls{PD} while the augmented reduced Hessian stays unchanged. 
The resulting \gls{QP} can thus keep the same primal solution if the active set is estimated correctly and the weight is large enough.
In the case where the augmented reduced Hessian is indefinite, or the estimation misses some active constraints or the penalty weight is too small,
the sweep can still reach a stage with negative curvature after adding the transferred cost and the penalty.
At such stages, it falls back to \gls{SQP}-level regularization by replacing the negative eigenvalues of the stage block 
with small positive values and continues the sweep, which modifies the \gls{QP} and thus its solution.

In the \gls{IPM}~\cite{nocedal2006numerical}, however,
the barrier term from the inequality constraints adds these curvatures inherently.
Since the curvature contribution increases on the constraints that are becoming active and vanishes on the inactive ones,
neither an estimate of the active set nor a penalty weight is needed.
When the augmented reduced Hessian is \gls{PD}, the barrier term makes the reduced Hessian \gls{PD} by itself.
As shown in the example of Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example}, the original reduced Hessian is indefinite.
\gls{SQP}-level regularization generally yields a direction towards an approximate solution,
whereas with the barrier term the reduced Hessian is \gls{PD}, 
so the \gls{IPM} produces a direction towards the exact solution.

    \caption{Newton step at an \gls{IPM} iterate of a \gls{QP} with indefinite reduced Hessian.}
    \label{fig:toy_example}
\end{figure}

In other cases, such as in the early iterations, 
where the augmented reduced Hessian fails to become \gls{PD}, a regularization to the Hessian is still needed.
But if the regularization is made inside the solver, 
the solution of the original \gls{QP} remains, and thus an exact Newton step in \gls{SQP} is preserved, 
which differs from the approximate Newton step obtained by \gls{SQP}-level regularization, 
and so does the feedback in real-time iteration.

A structure-exploiting interior point \gls{QP} solver that handles indefiniteness at the \gls{QP} level is 
therefore interesting as an alternative to regularizing at the \gls{SQP} level.
And this leaves open how the correction should be made inside the solver.
This thesis addresses this question for indefinite \glspl{QP} with optimal control structure.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
The main contributions of this thesis are the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Riccati-based regularization}: 
    we propose a regularization inside the Riccati recursion, i.e.\ Riccati-based regularization,
    that exploits the \gls{OCP} structure of the problem, and regularizes the Hessian only when Cholesky factorization fails.
    We further analyze why the minimum perturbation in this regularization fails,
    improve the regularization strategy,
    and prove the regularization to be inactive near the solution (Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Primal_Regularization}).

    \item \textbf{A benchmark of $175$ indefinite \gls{OCP} \glspl{QP}}:
    we provide a pipeline for collecting \glspl{QP} from the \gls{SQP} iterations with acados~\cite{verschueren2020acadosmodularopensourceframework},
    and collect $175$ \glspl{QP} from $8$ nonlinear \glspl{OCP},
    together with an analysis of the indefiniteness regarding the benchmark (Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Benchmark}).

    \item \textbf{Numerical comparison and ablation}:
    We implement a prototype solver that shows identical iteration behavior to HPIPM on convex \glspl{QP}, 
    and perform experiments with this solver on the benchmark to investigate the performance of the methods. 
    In addition, we present an analysis of solving failures 
    and include ablation studies on the solver settings to show which components the results depend on~(Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Outline}
\label{sec:outline}
The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.
In Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Theoretical_Background}, 
we start with theoretical background regarding the primal--dual \gls{IPM} for \gls{OCP}-structured \glspl{QP}, 
where the Riccati recursion is used to solve the \glspl{QP}, 
and introduce the barrier update and step size control strategies adopted in this work.
In Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Primal_Regularization} 
we propose the Riccati-based primal regularization,
derive the improved stage regularization strategy from the failure of minimum perturbation,
and prove that the regularization is asymptotically inactive.
Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Benchmark} constructs 
the benchmark of indefinite \glspl{QP} from the \gls{SQP} iterations of eight \glspl{OCP},
and an analysis is presented about how the indefiniteness is distributed over the stages and where it originates.
We carry out experiments in Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Experiments}.
We first describe the prototype solver and the experiment setup,
compare the Riccati-based methods with full Hessian regularization and inertia correction on the benchmark,
and discuss the instances on which the methods fail, with ablations for investigating which components the results depend on.
Finally, Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Conclusion} summarizes the results and outlines directions for future work.
