\chapter{Introduction}
\label{Chapter::Introduction}

In \gls{NMPC}, a nonlinear \gls{OCP} is solved in each sampling period 
to compute the optimal control input for the current state.
To solve the continuous-time \glspl{OCP}, 
direct multiple shooting~\cite{bock1984multiple} is a widely used method,
which transcribes the \gls{OCP} into a \gls{NLP} by discretizing the time horizon into multiple shooting intervals~\cite{Diehl2018}.
\Gls{SQP}~\cite{nocedal2006numerical} provides a suitable approach for solving the \gls{NLP},
which linearizes to form a \gls{QP} subproblem and solves it to obtain the search direction at each iterate.
In the real-time iteration~\cite{diehl2005real},
one \gls{SQP} iteration is performed per sampling time, with the linearization done before the new state is known,
so only the \gls{QP} solve lies between measurement and feedback.
The \gls{QP} solve therefore requires efficiency, for which structure-exploiting solvers are usually employed.
The open-source framework \texttt{acados}~\cite{verschueren2020acadosmodularopensourceframework}
combines the real-time iteration with such solvers.

When \gls{SQP} uses the exact Hessian of the Lagrangian, 
the resulting \gls{QP} subproblems might have an indefinite full Hessian.
However, structure-exploiting \gls{QP} solvers used within \gls{SQP}, 
such as qpDUNES~\cite{frasch2015parallel}, FORCES~\cite{domahidi2012efficient} and HPIPM~\cite{frison2020hpipmhighperformancequadraticprogramming}, 
may fail to solve these \glspl{QP}, since they require convex \glspl{QP} with a positive definite full Hessian.
One way to use these solvers in this situation is to regularize the full Hessian 
at the \gls{SQP} level before it is passed to the solver, while preserving the \gls{OCP} structure.
For this, \texttt{acados} provides several regularization methods, including two common ones, project and mirror~\cite{verschueren2017sparsity}.
Both methods regularize based on the eigenvalues of the full Hessian and use a small positive value as a threshold.
Project raises every eigenvalue to at least the threshold,
while mirror replaces the negative eigenvalues with their absolute values, using the threshold as a lower bound.
This, however, in general modifies the solution of the subproblems and also the feedback itself in the real-time iteration.

Convexification~\cite{verschueren2017sparsity}, as a more advanced regularization method, 
aims to keep the primal solution, and thus the feedback.
It exploits the fact that, if no inequality constraint is present, 
the primal solution is kept as long as the \emph{reduced Hessian}, 
i.e.\ the Hessian on the null space of the dynamics, is unchanged.
Otherwise, the same holds for the \emph{fully reduced Hessian},
i.e.\ the Hessian on the null space of the dynamics and the active constraints.
To this end, convexification applies a backward sweep similar to a Riccati recursion.
The sweep transfers cost between neighboring stages through the dynamics 
and at each stage adds curvature from the estimated active inequality constraints with an estimated weight.
The cost transfer keeps only the needed curvature for each stage to be positive definite 
while leaving the reduced Hessian unchanged.
The added curvature makes the reduced Hessian positive definite 
if the fully reduced Hessian is positive definite, 
which makes a correct estimation of the active constraints and the weight possible, 
and if this estimation is indeed correct.
The full Hessian is then positive definite with the fully reduced Hessian unchanged,
so the original primal solution is kept.
But if the fully reduced Hessian is indefinite or the estimation is incorrect, 
the sweep could still reach a stage with indefinite blocks,
where convexification falls back to projection, which modifies the \gls{QP} solution.
In \texttt{acados}, convexification implements only the cost transfer
and could thus fail to keep the primal solution with inequality constraints in practice.

In an interior-point \gls{QP} solver, however,
the \gls{IPM} relaxes the complementarity from each inequality constraint by a barrier parameter.
This inherently adds weighted curvature from inequality constraints to the Hessian throughout the iterations.
The weight on each constraint, i.e.\ the ratio of its multiplier to its slack, changes with the current iterate,
so neither an estimate of the active set nor a penalty weight is needed.
The reduced Hessian with curvature from inequality constraints can be positive definite,
as long as the fully reduced Hessian is positive definite.

\begin{example}[A \gls{QP} with an indefinite reduced Hessian]
\label{ex:toy}
Consider the following indefinite \gls{QP}
\begin{mini}
  {x_0,u_0,x_1,u_1,x_2}
  {\tfrac{1}{2}(u_0^2 - 3x_1^2 + u_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}x_2^2)}
  {\label{eq:toy_qp}}
  {}
  \addConstraint{x_0}{=1}
  \addConstraint{x_{1}}{=x_0 + u_0}
  \addConstraint{x_{2}}{=x_1 + u_1}
  \addConstraint{-1}{\le u_0 \le 1.}{}
\end{mini}
By eliminating $x_0$, $x_1$ and $x_2$ using the equality constraints,
we have a \gls{QP} with an indefinite reduced Hessian as:
\begin{mini}
  {u_0,u_1}
  {\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{3}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}
   + \begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{5}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}}
  {\label{eq:toy_qp_reduced}}
  {}
  \addConstraint{-1}{\le u_0 \le 1.}{}
\end{mini}
Denote the objective of~\eqref{eq:toy_qp_reduced} by $f(u_0,u_1)$.
For a fixed $u_0$, the minimizer of the objective over $u_1$ follows from
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial u_1} = \tfrac{1}{2}u_0 + \tfrac{3}{2}u_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  u_1^\star(u_0) = -\tfrac{1}{3}(u_0+1).
\end{equation*}
Substituting $u_1^\star(u_0)$ back into $f$ leaves a function of $u_0$ alone,
\begin{equation*}
  f\bigl(u_0,u_1^\star(u_0)\bigr) = -\tfrac{5}{6}u_0^2 - \tfrac{8}{3}u_0 - \tfrac{1}{12}.
\end{equation*}
Since $f\bigl(u_0,u_1^\star(u_0)\bigr)$ is concave, its minimum over $-1 \le u_0 \le 1$ lies on a bound,
and comparing $f\bigl(-1, 0\bigr) = \tfrac{7}{4}$ with $f\bigl(1,-\frac{2}{3}\bigr)= -\tfrac{43}{12}$ gives
the exact solution $(1, -\frac{2}{3})$.

The contour lines of the reduced objective are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_sqp}a
with a saddle point in reduced space and the exact solution lying at $(1, -\frac{2}{3})$ on the constraint boundary.

From an \gls{SQP} perspective, 
this \gls{QP} can occur either as a subproblem within the SQP iterations 
or as an \gls{NLP} that is then solved using \gls{SQP}.

In the former case, the \gls{SQP}-level regularization, here mirror, 
modifies the \gls{QP} before it is passed to the \gls{QP} solver.
With decision variables as $(x_0,u_0,x_1,u_1,x_2)$,
the full Hessian of~\eqref{eq:toy_qp} is $\operatorname{diag}\bigl(0, 1, -3, 1, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$.
Mirror replaces the eigenvalue $-3$ by $3$ and lifts $0$ to a threshold, here $10^{-4}$,
resulting in the regularized full Hessian as $\operatorname{diag}\bigl(10^{-4}, 1, 3, 1, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$.
Eliminating the states as before gives a subproblem with a positive definite reduced Hessian
\begin{mini*}|l|
  {u_0,u_1}
  {\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{9}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}
   + \begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{7}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}\!
   \begin{bmatrix}u_0\\u_1\end{bmatrix}}
  {}{}
  \addConstraint{-1}{\le u_0 \le 1,}{}
\end{mini*}
and the corresponding solution is $(-\tfrac{10}{13}, -\tfrac{1}{13})$, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_sqp}b.
This solution is an approximate solution for the \gls{QP} subproblem 
and also an approximate Newton step for \gls{SQP}.

For completeness, Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_sqp}c shows the latter case, 
where \gls{SQP} solves~\eqref{eq:toy_qp} as an \gls{NLP}.
\Gls{SQP} regularizes and linearizes at each iterate.
Since the problem is a \gls{QP}, the regularized Hessian stays constant and only the gradient changes.
From the cold start, \gls{SQP} first takes the approximate step given by the first subproblem
and then takes several \gls{SQP} iterations to reach the exact solution, 
which is the fixed point satisfying the optimality conditions of~\eqref{eq:toy_qp}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{images/introduction/toy_bound_sqp_level.pdf}
    \caption{
    Contour lines of a QP with an indefinite reduced Hessian, 
    coloured from blue (low) to yellow (high) objective value within each panel.
    (a) shows the original objective, 
    (b) shows the subproblem objective of the \gls{QP} after mirror,
    and (c) shows the \gls{SQP} iterates when the \gls{QP} is solved as an \acrshort{NLP} for completeness.}
    \label{fig:toy_example_sqp}
\end{figure}
From a \gls{QP} perspective, 
an \gls{IPM} can exploit the curvature contributions from inequality constraints, 
as detailed later in Section~\ref{sec:ipm}.
At the cold start, both bounds in $-1 \le u_0 \le 1$ have the slack as $1$.
With the initial barrier parameter chosen as $10$,
the corresponding initialized multiplier is $10$, so the summed weight of two constraints is $20$.
Since both bounds act on $u_0$ only, it adds to the $u_0$ entry of the reduced Hessian,
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{3}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}
  + \begin{bmatrix}20 & 0\\[2pt] 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{37}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation*}
which is positive definite.
The local second-order model in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_ipm}b is therefore convex without any extra work.
The \gls{IPM} can thus solve the indefinite \gls{QP} and obtain the exact solution in several \gls{QP} iterations, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_example_ipm}c.

    Contour lines of a QP with an indefinite reduced Hessian, 
    coloured from blue (low) to yellow (high) objective value within each panel.
    (a) shows the original objective, 
    (b) shows the local second-order model with curvature contributions from inequality constraints,
    and (c) shows the \acrshort{IPM} iterates on the \gls{QP}.}
    \label{fig:toy_example_ipm}
\end{figure}

\end{example}

In other cases, such as in the early \gls{SQP} iterations,
where the curvature contribution may not be enough to make the reduced Hessian positive definite, 
a regularization to the Hessian is still needed.
But if the regularization is performed inside the solver, 
the solution of the original \gls{QP} remains, and thus an exact Newton step in \gls{SQP} is preserved.

A structure-exploiting interior-point \gls{QP} solver that handles indefiniteness at the \gls{QP} level is 
therefore interesting as an alternative to regularizing at the \gls{SQP} level.
This consideration leads to the question of how the regularization should be performed inside the solver,
and this thesis addresses this question for indefinite \glspl{QP} with \gls{OCP} structure.

\paragraph{Contributions}
The main contributions of this thesis are the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Riccati-based regularization}: 
    This thesis investigates regularizations inside interior-point \gls{QP} solvers.
    The main focus is the proposed Riccati-based regularization,
    which regularizes only when Cholesky factorization fails during the Riccati recursion.
    The thesis further analyzes why the minimum perturbation in this regularization fails,
    improves the regularization strategy, 
    and proves that the Riccati-based regularization is inactive near the solution (Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Primal_Regularization}).

    \item \textbf{A benchmark of $170$ indefinite \gls{OCP} \glspl{QP}}:
    This thesis provides a pipeline for collecting \glspl{QP} from \gls{SQP} iterations performed within \texttt{acados},
    and collects $170$ \glspl{QP} from $8$ nonlinear \glspl{OCP},
    together with an analysis of the indefiniteness regarding the benchmark (Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Benchmark}).

    \item \textbf{Numerical comparison and ablation}:
    This thesis implements a prototype solver in Python that shows identical iteration behavior to HPIPM on convex \glspl{QP}, 
    and performs experiments with this solver on the benchmark to investigate the performance of all regularizations. 
    In addition, this thesis also presents an analysis of solving failures 
    and includes ablation studies on the solver settings to show which components the results depend on~(Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Outline}
The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.
In Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Theoretical_Background}, 
we introduce the theoretical background on primal--dual \glspl{IPM} for \gls{OCP}-structured \glspl{QP},
where the Riccati recursion solves the linear system in each iteration.
We also present the barrier update and step size control schemes adopted in this thesis.
In Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Primal_Regularization},
we propose the Riccati-based primal regularization,
derive the improved stage-wise regularization strategy from the failure of minimum perturbation,
and prove that the regularization is asymptotically inactive.
In Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Benchmark}, we present the benchmark of indefinite \glspl{QP} constructed from the \gls{SQP} iterations of eight \glspl{OCP},
and analyze how the indefiniteness is distributed over the stages and where it originates.
In Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Experiments}, we describe the prototype solver and the experiment setup,
and report the numerical results.
The experiments compare the Riccati-based regularizations with full Hessian regularization and inertia correction on the benchmark,
and discuss the instances on which the methods fail, with ablations for investigating which components the results depend on.
Finally, in Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Conclusion}, we summarize the results and outline directions for future work.
