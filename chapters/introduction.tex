\chapter{Introduction}
\label{Chapter::Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

In the field of nonlinear model predictive control (NMPC),
in each sampling period, 
a nonlinear optimal control problem (OCP) is solved to compute the optimal control input for the current state of the system.
In order to solve the continuous-time optimal control problems,
direct multiple shooting~\cite{bock1984multiple} is a widely used method,
which transcribes the OCP into a nonlinear programming problem (NLP) by 
discretizing the time horizon into multiple shooting intervals~\cite{Diehl2018}.

Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) provides a suitable approach for solving the NLP in real-time practice,
which linearizes at each iterate to generate a QP subproblem.
In the real-time iteration~\cite{diehl2005real},
one SQP iteration is performed per sampling time, warm-started at the shifted previous solution and linearized before the new state is known,
so that only the QP solve remains between measurement and feedback.
The solving of the QP subproblem therefore determines the feedback delay,
and it can be solved by structure-exploiting solver such as HPIPM~\cite{frison2020hpipmhighperformancequadraticprogramming}, 
which runs Interior Point Method (IPM) for solving QPs。

Nevertheless, when the full Hessian of the QP subproblems is provided by the exact Hessian of the Lagrangian \cite{BoggsTolle1995}, 
it could be indefinite, thus indicates a nonconvex problem.
The structure-exploiting solvers embedded in the SQP,
such as qpDUNES~\cite{frasch2015parallel}, FORCES~\cite{domahidi2012efficient} and HPIPM, 
however, fails to solve these QPs, for they require convex QPs with positive definite (PD) full Hessian.
Though FORCES and HPIPM can in principle handle the nonconvex QPs with indefinite full Hessian as long as the reduced Hessian,
i.e. the Hessian on the null space of the dynamics, is PD,
but the code optimization of both solvers still requires a PD full Hessian, thus convex QPs are intended by the solvers instead of nonconvex QPs.
One solution to use these solvers in this situation is to regularize at the SQP level, 
i.e.\ to regularize the full Hessian of the QP before it is passed to the solver,
which in general modifies the solution of the subproblems and in the real-time iteration the feedback itself.

Given the Hessian on the null space of the dynamics and the active constraints is PD,
CONVEXITY~\cite{verschueren2017sparsity} can avoid the modification when the full Hessian is not PD.
It applies a backward sweep similar to Riccati recursion to shift curvature between neighboring stages,
and adds curvature with a tunable penalty on the estimated active constraints outside the null space of the dynamics,
which regularizes the full Hessian without touching the reduced Hessian.
And the resulting QP keeps the same primal solution if the estimation is correct.
In the case where both Hessian matrices are indefinite, 
it falls back to SQP level regularization and thereby modifies the QP by
replacing the negative eigenvalues of the indefinite full Hessian stage-wisely by small positive values.

In an interior point method, however,
the barrier term of the inequality constraints adds this curvature by itself.
Since the curvature contribution increases on the constraints that is becoming active and vanishes on the inactive ones,
estimation on the active set and tuning on the penalty weight can be avoided.
Especially when the iterates are close enough to the solution on the constraint,
the barrier term can make the reduced Hessian PD without the solver knowing which constraints are active.
In the early iterations, or where this condition fails, the Hessian still has to be corrected.
If a correction inside the solver is made after the barrier curvature has been added,
unlike a correction at the SQP level, 
it only has to supply the curvature that the barrier lacks and changes only the Newton step, 
while the residuals, and hence the found solution, remain those of the original QP.

A structure-exploiting interior point QP solver that handles indefiniteness itself is 
therefore interesting as an alternative to regularization at the SQP level.
This leaves open how the correction should be made inside the Riccati recursion.
This thesis addresses this question for indefinite QPs with optimal control structure.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
The main contributions of this thesis are the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A regularization inside the Riccati recursion} of an interior point QP solver,
    which corrects the Hessian only at the stages where it is needed and is inactive near the solution,
    together with an improved stage-wise strategy (Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Primal_Regularization}).

    \item \textbf{A benchmark of $175$ indefinite OCP QPs} collected from the SQP iterations of eight OCPs,
    with an analysis of where the indefiniteness comes from (Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Benchmark}).

    \item \textbf{A numerical comparison} on this benchmark, where the methods are compared for performance
    from the efficiency and estimated computation perspectives, and an analysis of solving failures is presented. 
    (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related open-source solvers}
\label{sec:related_solvers}
This thesis builds on two open-source software packages for embedded optimal control, acados and HPIPM.

acados~\cite{verschueren2020acadosmodularopensourceframework} is a modular framework for fast NMPC.
It transcribes the OCP by direct multiple shooting, solves the resulting NLP by SQP or its real-time iteration variant,
and passes the QP subproblems to one of several interfaced QP solvers.
When the exact Hessian is used, acados handles indefiniteness at the SQP level:
the QP Hessian can be regularized before the QP is passed to the solver or be replaced by a Gauss--Newton approximation.
In this thesis, acados is used to generate the SQP iterates from which the benchmark QPs are collected (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_construction}).

HPIPM~\cite{frison2020hpipmhighperformancequadraticprogramming} is a primal--dual interior point solver
for OCP-structured QPs and the default QP solver in acados.
It solves the KKT system of each iteration by Riccati recursion, with a cost linear in the horizon length,
and relies on high-performance linear algebra routines tailored to the small and medium-sized matrices of optimal control.
HPIPM assumes a convex QP: the Riccati recursion requires the reduced Hessian blocks to be positive definite, so it fails on indefinite QPs.
This failure is precisely the criterion by which the benchmark QPs are collected (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_construction}),
and the prototype solver used in the experiments reproduces the iteration behavior of HPIPM on convex QPs, 
so that the regularization is the only difference between the two (Section~\ref{sec:prototype}).


\section{Outline}
\label{sec:outline}
The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.
Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Theoretical_Background} introduces the primal--dual interior point method for OCP-structured QPs,
the Riccati recursion used to solve its KKT system,
and the barrier update and step size control strategies adopted in the solver.
Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Primal_Regularization} classifies primal regularization by its target matrix,
develops the Riccati-based primal regularization,
derives the improved stage regularization strategy from the failure of minimum perturbation,
and proves that the regularization is asymptotically inactive.
Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Benchmark} constructs the benchmark of indefinite QPs from the SQP iterations of eight OCPs
and analyzes how the indefiniteness is distributed over the stages and where it originates.
Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Experiments} describes the prototype solver and the experiment setup,
compares the Riccati-based methods with full Hessian regularization and inertia correction on the benchmark,
and discusses the instances on which the methods fail.
Finally, Chapter~\ref{Chapter::Conclusion} summarizes the results and outlines directions for future work.
