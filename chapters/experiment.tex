\section{Experiment Metrics}
    We evaluate each regularization strategy along two complementary axes: 
    how many iterations it takes to converge, and how much arithmetic each iteration costs. 
    The former is captured by the \emph{iteration profile}, 
    the latter by expressing all dense linear-algebra work in a common \emph{flop unit}.

    \subsection{Iteration Profile}
    Let $\mathcal{P}$ denote the set of test instances and $\mathcal{M}$ the set
    of candidate regularization strategies. For a method $m \in \mathcal{M}$ and
    an instance $p \in \mathcal{P}$, let $i_{m,p}$ be the number of outer
    iterations at which $m$ satisfies the convergence criterion on $p$. A run
    that reaches the iteration cap $i_{\max}$ without converging, or that stalls
    early, is treated as unsolved and we set $i_{m,p}=\infty$.

    The \emph{iteration profile} of method $m$ is
    \begin{equation}
    \pi_m(k) \;=\; \bigl|\{\, p \in \mathcal{P} : i_{m,p} \le k \,\}\bigr|,
    \qquad k = 1,\dots,i_{\max},
    \end{equation}
    i.e.\ the number of instances that $m$ solves within a budget of $k$
    iterations. Equivalently, $\pi_m(k)/|\mathcal{P}|$ is the empirical
    cumulative distribution of the solve-iteration count over solved instances.
    By construction $\pi_m$ is non-decreasing in $k$, and its value at the cap,
    $\pi_m(i_{\max})$, equals the total number of instances solved by $m$ under
    the budget.

    The profile is read along two axes. The rate at which $\pi_m(k)$ rises with
    $k$ reflects the \emph{efficiency} of method $m$: a curve that climbs
    steeply at small $k$ solves most instances with few iterations. The height
    of the plateau, $\pi_m(i_{\max})$, reflects the \emph{robustness} of the
    method: it is the fraction of the test set solved within the prescribed
    budget. Because every method is evaluated under the same cap $i_{\max}$ and
    the same convergence criterion, the curves are directly comparable across
    $\mathcal{M}$.

    This metric gives a summary of how reliably and how quickly each strategy converges across the test set.

    \subsection{Flop Unit Estimation}
    Iteration counts alone do not reflect the work performed per iteration,
    which differs substantially across regularization strategies: an
    eigenvalue-based correction and a modified-Cholesky correction may reach
    convergence in a comparable number of iterations while incurring very
    different arithmetic cost. To compare methods on a common scale, we express
    all dense linear-algebra work in a single flop unit.

    We take one modified Cholesky factorization of the block reduced Hessian as
    the unit. For a control dimension $n_u$, factorizing an $n_u \times n_u$
    matrix costs approximately $\tfrac{1}{3} n_u^3$ flops, hence
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{unit} \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{3}\, n_u^3 .
    \end{equation}
    The modified factorization differs from the plain one only by lower-order
    diagonal updates, so to leading order it also costs one unit.

    The remaining primitives are expressed relative to this unit. A symmetric
    eigenvalue decomposition of the same block costs about $9 n_u^3$ flops
    \cite{verschuerenTimeoptimalRaceCar2016b}, i.e.
    \begin{equation}
    \frac{9 n_u^3}{\tfrac{1}{3} n_u^3} \;=\; 27 \ \text{units}.
    \end{equation}

    With this unit defined, the arithmetic cost of a solve, including the regularization applied at each iteration,
    can be expressed on a common scale and accumulated over the iteration profile.

    \subsection{General setup}
    With the Python prototype implemented, 
    the following regularization methods in the solver are considered when reduced Hessian is indefinite:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{PROJECT\_H}: Do Project to the full Hessian
        \item \texttt{MIRROR\_H}: Do Mirror to the full Hessian
        \item \texttt{PRECOMPUTE}: Compute regularized full Hessian before Schur complement and reuse it, avoiding per-iteration correction.
        \item \texttt{PM\_STAGE\_MIRROR}: eigenvalue mirroring applied per stage to each stagewise block (introduced in this work).
        \item \texttt{PM\_STAGE\_GMW}: modified Cholesky (Gill--Murray--Wright) applied per stage to each stagewise block (introduced in this work).
        \item \texttt{INERTIA\_LM}: inertia-controlled Levenberg--Marquardt regularization, using the same update parameters as IPOPT (introduced in this work).
    \end{itemize}

    We set the maximal iteration $i_\mathrm{max}$ as 100. For convergence criterion, 
    we have $1\mathrm{e}-6$ for stationary residual $r_\mathrm{stat}$ and complementarity residual $r_\mathrm{comp}$, 
    $1\mathrm{e}-8$ for equality residual $r_\mathrm{eq}$ and inequality residual $r_\mathrm{ineq}$ .

\section{Numerical Results}
    In this section, we present the numerical results of the proposed regularization methods on nonconvex QPs with different solvers.
    We default Mehrotra's probing as the barrier update strategy, the proposed funnel line search as the step size control strategy, 
    and compare the iteration profile and flop unit consumption of different regularization methods.

    \subsection{Regularizations Comparison}
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/experiment/iteration_qp_results_riccati.pdf}
        \caption{Iteration profile on the indefinite QP dataset (155 problems)
        with funnel line search: for each method, the number of problems solved
        within a given iteration budget (100 iterations).}
        \label{fig:iter-profile-search}
    \end{figure}

        Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-search} reports the iteration profile on the indefinite QP dataset, 
    where a curve that rises higher and further to the left corresponds to a method that solves more problems within a smaller iteration budget. 
    Without any regularization the solver stalls almost immediately and solves only about $29$ of the $155$ problems, 
    on the one hand this confirms the need for regularization for the interior-point iteration to make progress in indefinite QPs,
    on the other hand it also shows a common situation where the initial indefinite reduced Hessian becomes definite due to contribution from the constraints.
    Among the regularized variants, PM\_STAGE\_MIRROR is the strongest of the proposed schemes and essentially matches the IPOPT reference,
    both eventually solving the entire set of $155$ problems. 
    PM\_STAGE\_GMW and INERTIA\_LM follow closely, solving $150$ and $147$ problems respectively, 
    while the full-Hessian strategies PROJECT\_H and MIRROR\_H, together with PRECOMPUTE, remain clearly below, solving $132$, $126$ and $137$ problems 
    within the same budget. 
    In terms of robustness, the stage-wise family therefore dominates the naive full-Hessian regularizations.

    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/experiment/decomp_flop_worstcase_qp_results.pdf}
        \caption{Distribution of the per-problem linear-algebra cost, measured in defined units on a logarithmic scale. 
        Each box is annotated with the worst-case cost and the number of failed problems.}
        \label{fig:flop-box-search}
    \end{figure}

    Figure~\ref{fig:flop-box-search} complements this picture with the computational cost, 
    expressed in modified-Cholesky units on a logarithmic scale.
    Two observations stand out. First, the ordering by number of failures mirrors the iteration profile: 
    PM\_STAGE\_MIRROR does not fail on any problem,
    PM\_STAGE\_GMW and INERTIA\_LM fail on only $5$ and $8$ problems, 
    whereas PROJECT\_H, MIRROR\_H and PRECOMPUTE fail on $23$, $29$ and $18$ problems respectively. 
    Second, and more importantly, the stage-wise factorization-based schemes are also by far the cheapest: 
    PM\_STAGE\_GMW and INERTIA\_LM concentrate around $10^{3}$ units with a tight spread, 
    roughly two orders of magnitude below PROJECT\_H and MIRROR\_H, 
    whose medians lie near $10^{5}$--$10^{6}$ units. 
    PM\_STAGE\_MIRROR sits in between: it has a low median but a heavy upper tail (worst case $9.1\times10^{5}$ units), 
    reflecting the cost of the stage-wise eigendecomposition, 
    which alone accounts for roughly $27$ factorization units whenever a block is modified.

    \subsection{Ablation: without Funnel Line Search}

    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/experiment/iteration_qp_results_riccati_nosearch.pdf}
        \caption{Iteration profile on the indefinite QP dataset (155 problems)
        with a plain FTB step-size rule instead of the funnel line search.}
        \label{fig:iter-profile-nosearch}
    \end{figure}
    
    To isolate the contribution of the funnel line search, we repeat the experiment
    with a plain FTB step-size rule and keep every other component of the solver unchanged. 
    Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-nosearch} shows the resulting iteration profile. 
    The overall ranking is preserved,
    the stage-wise schemes still dominate the full-Hessian regularizations and NO\_REGULARIZE still stalls
    immediately,
    but each well-behaved method solves fewer problems within the iteration budget than with the funnel line search.
    
    \begin{table}[H]
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{lcc}
            \toprule
            Method & with line search & without line search \\
            \midrule
            PROJECT\_H & 23 & 24 \\
            MIRROR\_H & 29 & 35 \\
            PM\_STAGE\_MIRROR & 0 & 5 \\
            PM\_STAGE\_GMW & 5 & 11 \\
            INERTIA\_LM & 8 & 12 \\
            PRECOMPUTE & 18 & 17 \\
            \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
        \caption{Number of failed problems (out of 155) with and without the funnel
        line search.}
        \label{tab:ablation-fail}
    \end{table}
    
    Table~\ref{tab:ablation-fail} makes this precise. 
    The funnel line search reduces the number of failures for exactly the methods that already produce good search directions: 
    PM\_STAGE\_MIRROR drops from $5$ to $0$ failures, PM\_STAGE\_GMW from $11$ to $5$, 
    INERTIA\_LM from $12$ to $8$, and MIRROR\_H from $35$ to $29$. 
    In contrast, PROJECT\_H and PRECOMPUTE are essentially unaffected ($24\to23$ and $17\to18$). 
    This is consistent with the role of the line search: it turns near-misses into convergent runs by controlling step acceptance, 
    but it cannot repair the fundamentally poor directions produced by the full-Hessian minimal-perturbation strategy, 
    whose failures stem from the regularized Hessian matrices rather than from the step size.

    The per-problem linear-algebra cost, by contrast, is left essentially unchanged:
    the box positions, spreads and worst-case values coincide with those of Figure~\ref{fig:flop-box-search} almost exactly, 
    because the funnel line search alters which steps are accepted, 
    not the factorization performed at each iteration. 
    We therefore attribute the robustness of the full solver to the combination
    of stage-wise regularization and funnel line search, with the line search contributing robustness at no additional linear-algebra cost.
