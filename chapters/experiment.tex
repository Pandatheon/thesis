\chapter{Numerical experiments} % Overview text
\label{Chapter::Experiments}
In this chapter, we evaluate the proposed regularization methods numerically and discuss the results.
We begin in Section~\ref{sec:metrics} with the metrics used throughout the comparison:
a proxy unit for the factorization cost and the absolute performance profile, which compares the methods in both iteration count and factorization cost.
Section~\ref{sec:prototype} then describes the prototype solver on which all experiments are run 
and stresses that it reproduces the iteration behavior of HPIPM on convex \glspl{QP}, 
so that any difference observed later can be attributed to the regularization rather than to the solver itself.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} fixes the experiment setup, 
naming and classifying the regularization methods under comparison and stating the parameters shared by all of them.
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} presents the numerical comparison, 
first among the Riccati-based methods themselves and then against full Hessian regularization and inertia correction,
followed by ablations of the barrier update and the line search.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} concludes the chapter by analyzing the failures and the Mehrotra ablation.

\section{Experiment metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
In this section, we introduce the metrics used to evaluate the performance of different regularization strategies in our experiments.
Since we care about both the convergence behavior and the factorization cost caused by different regularization methods,
we measure the cost of a run in two ways: by its iteration count and by its factorization cost.
For the latter, Section~\ref{subsec:flop_unit} defines a proxy unit based on one Cholesky factorization of the reduced Hessian block.
Section~\ref{subsec:performance_profile} then introduces the absolute performance profile used to compare the methods.

\subsection{Proxy unit for factorization cost}
\label{subsec:flop_unit}
The regularization strategies differ in the factorizations they perform per iteration.
Some use an eigenvalue decomposition, some a modified Cholesky factorization,
and some repeat the Cholesky factorization with an increasing diagonal shift.
To make the factorization costs comparable, we express them in a common proxy unit.

We take one Cholesky factorization of the reduced Hessian block $\tilde{M}_k$ as the proxy unit. 
For factorizing an $n_u \times n_u$ matrix, the cost is approximately $\tfrac{1}{3} n_u^3$ flops, hence
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{unit} \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{3}\, n_u^3.
\end{equation}
Other factorizations are expressed relative to this unit.

A modified Cholesky factorization of an $n_u \times n_u$ matrix
only adds diagonal updates of $\mathcal{O}(n_u^2)$ cost, 
so to leading order it also costs $1$ unit.
A symmetric eigenvalue decomposition of the same dimension matrix costs 
about $9 n_u^3$ flops~\cite{verschuerenTimeoptimalRaceCar2016b},
which gives $9 n_u^3 / (\tfrac{1}{3} n_u^3) = 27$ units.

For matrices of other dimensions, such as
the cost-to-go matrix $P_k$ of dimension $n_x \times n_x$ 
and a full Hessian block $\tilde{H}_k$ of dimension ${(n_x+n_u) \times (n_x+n_u)}$, 
the ratios are taken as
\begin{equation}
\omega_P \;=\; \left(\frac{n_x}{n_u}\right)^{3}, \quad
\omega_H \;=\; \left(\frac{n_x+n_u}{n_u}\right)^{3}.
\end{equation}

The factorization cost of a run is thus the sum of two parts,
the Cholesky factorizations of the Riccati recursion itself and
the additional factorizations introduced by the regularization.
The latter includes every rerun of the Riccati recursion, whose factorizations are counted again,
and every failed Cholesky factorization, which counts as one proxy unit, although it may stop early.

Three points should be noted.
Since the full Hessian regularization modifies all $N$ blocks $\tilde{H}_k$ 
and the terminal block $\tilde{Q}_N = P_N$ at once, its cost scales with $N$.
The factorization cost omits the triangular solves and matrix products of the factorization step,
and thus favors regularizations that repeat this step, such as inertia correction.
Moreover, since the recursion requires the Cholesky factor of the reduced Hessian block,
a modified Cholesky factorization of this block provides the factor directly, 
whereas an eigenvalue modification requires an additional Cholesky factorization.
Table~\ref{tab:flop_units} summarizes the additional factorization cost of either regularization applied to different matrices.

\subsection{Absolute performance profile}
\label{subsec:performance_profile}
In our experiments, we use a variant of the performance profile~\cite{dolanBenchmarkingOptimizationSoftware2002},
which we call the \emph{absolute performance profile}.
Instead of the fraction of instances solved within a factor of the best method's cost,
it plots the number of instances a method solves within a certain cost on the vertical axis against the absolute cost on the horizontal axis.
The profile of a method thus does not depend on the other methods and emphasizes the method's own ability to solve the instances.

We use two cost measures, which give two profiles:
the \emph{iteration profile}, which measures the cost by the iteration count,
and the \emph{proxy unit profile}, which measures it by the total factorization cost in the proxy units of Section~\ref{subsec:flop_unit}.
An instance that the method fails to solve within the maximum iteration count $i_\mathrm{max}$ counts as unsolved in both profiles.
A curve that rises steeply at low cost indicates an \emph{efficient} method, in iterations or in factorization,
and its final value, the number of solved instances, measures \emph{robustness} and is the same in both profiles.

\section{Prototype solver implementation}
\label{sec:prototype}
To ensure a fair comparison, we implemented all regularization methods in one prototype solver.
The Riccati-based primal--dual \gls{IPM} prototype solver is implemented in Python, using HPIPM as a reference.
The prototype was designed to reproduce its iteration behavior on convex \glspl{QP} and is available at \url{https://github.com/Pandatheon/Primal-dual_IPM.git}.

One implementation detail deserves a special mention, 
as it deviates from the plain \gls{FTB} rule stated in Section~\ref{sec:size_control}. 
Rather than being a fixed fraction $\eta$ of the maximal step size,
the primal and dual step sizes $\alpha^{\mathrm{prim}}$ and $\alpha^{\mathrm{dual}}$ are obtained 
from their respective maximal step sizes through the heuristic
\begin{equation}
  \alpha =
  \begin{cases}
    \alpha^{\mathrm{max}}\big[(1-\alpha^{\mathrm{max}})\,\kappa_\eta + \alpha^{\mathrm{max}}\hat{\eta}\big], & \alpha^{\mathrm{max}} < 1,\\
    \alpha^{\mathrm{max}}, & \alpha^{\mathrm{max}} = 1,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha^{\mathrm{max}}\in(0,1]$ denotes the maximal step size from \eqref{eq:ftb_max_prim} or \eqref{eq:ftb_max_dual},
$\kappa_\eta = 0.99$ and $\hat{\eta} = 1-10^{-7}$ as in HPIPM.

When $\alpha^{\mathrm{max}} < 1$, 
the bracketed factor changes with $\alpha^{\mathrm{max}}$, 
from a strongly blocked step to a nearly full one, so the closer the maximal step is to a full step, the less it is shortened.
When $\alpha^{\mathrm{max}} = 1$,
an unblocked full step can be taken without scaling by $\hat{\eta}$ so that
the local quadratic convergence rate of the \gls{IPM} can be preserved.

We further align the prototype solver with HPIPM in terms of the mode settings by defining a set of HPIPM settings, 
referred to as the presetting in the following, whose values are listed in Table~\ref{tab:customized_settings}.

As one can notice, the presetting largely coincides with the \texttt{SPEED} mode of HPIPM, 
differing in three respects. 
First, the square-root algorithm is disabled (\texttt{square\_root\_alg}$=0$)
so that the Riccati recursion requires no positive definite full Hessian to carry on.
Second, the primal and dual step sizes are computed separately (\texttt{split\_step}$=1$) 
instead of sharing a single step length.
Third, the minimal step size is disabled by setting \texttt{alpha\_min}$=0$.
In both modes no iterative refinement is applied (\texttt{itref\_pred\_max}$=$\texttt{itref\_corr\_max}$=0$) 
and the conditional Mehrotra's predictor--corrector is retained (\texttt{pred\_corr}$=$\texttt{cond\_pred\_corr}$=1$).

To verify that the two implementations do agree, we collected $40$ convex \gls{QP} subproblems, 
five from each of the $8$ \glspl{OCP} in the benchmark, 
and compared the iteration behavior of the prototype solver against that of HPIPM with the presetting. 
The resulting iteration profiles are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:convex_iteration_profile}. 

Both solvers solve all $40$ instances in the exact same number of iterations for each instance, 
resulting in iteration profiles that coincide.
On convex \glspl{QP}, the prototype solver therefore reproduces the iteration behavior of HPIPM, 
which verifies the prototype as a reliable baseline and allows us to attribute the differences observed in the experiments to the regularization 
rather than to the solver.

\section{Experiment setup}
\label{sec:setup}
In this section, 
the settings for the experiments are introduced.
First, based on the previously discussed methods, 
we name and classify the regularization methods considered in the experiment in Section~\ref{subsec:experimented_regularizations}, 
and then we introduce the general setup of the experiments in Section~\ref{subsec:general_setup}, 
including the convergence criterion and step size control scheme.

\subsection{Evaluated regularizations}
\label{subsec:experimented_regularizations}
Before turning to the results, we fix the set of regularization methods entering the comparison, 
together with the naming convention used throughout this chapter.

Every method is named in the form \textsf{TARGET\_STRATEGY}.
The \textsf{TARGET} indicates the matrix or matrices from which the correction is derived and to which it is applied,
which gives the three categories of \emph{full Hessian regularization}, \emph{Riccati-based regularization}, and \emph{inertia correction}.
The \textsf{STRATEGY} is one of the numerical strategies of Section~\ref{subsec:reg_strategies},
which are largely independent of the matrix they act on and can therefore be paired with each target.

\paragraph{Full Hessian regularization.}
The first category follows Section~\ref{subsec:full_hessian} and takes the full Hessian as the target matrix.
The regularization is triggered by the failure of the Cholesky factorization in the Riccati recursion, 
and is applied stage-wise to each full Hessian block.
The Riccati recursion is then re-attempted with the regularized full Hessian.
Applying the three strategies of Section~\ref{subsec:reg_strategies} to each block $\tilde{H}_k$
gives \textsf{H\_PROJECT}, \textsf{H\_MIRROR} and \textsf{H\_GMW}.

\paragraph{Riccati-based regularization.}
The second category is the one proposed in Section~\ref{sec:riccati_primal_reg} and acts on the reduced Hessian $\tilde{M}$.
The algorithms are given in Algorithms~\ref{alg:modified_riccati_recursion} and \ref{alg:improved_modified_riccati_recursion},
which differ in which matrices of the backward pass the adjustment is derived from and applied to, 
giving two targets within this category:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textsf{M}: only the reduced Hessian block $\tilde{M}_k$ is corrected, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:modified_riccati_recursion}. 
  The correction is purely local and serves as a minimal safeguard for the factorization.
  \item \textsf{PM}: the cost-to-go matrix $P_{k+1}$ entering the stage is corrected as well, 
  as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:improved_modified_riccati_recursion} introduced in this work, 
  so that the analyzed propagation is taken into account.
\end{itemize}

Pairing each target with the three strategies gives six methods,
which correspond to the two insights of Section~\ref{sec:fail_minimal_perturbation}.
\textsf{M\_PROJECT} applies neither of the two and serves as the reference, 
\textsf{M\_MIRROR} and \textsf{M\_GMW} apply only the first insight, i.e.\ non-minimum perturbation,
\textsf{PM\_PROJECT} applies only the second, which considers the propagation with minimum perturbation,
and \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} apply both.

\paragraph{Inertia correction.}
The third category is the Levenberg--Marquardt-style inertia correction of Section~\ref{subsec:reg_strategies},
which we denote by \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.

This category is distinguished from the two preceding categories, as it has no distinct target matrix in the above sense.
In this category, a scalar multiple of the identity $\delta_w I$ is added to each full Hessian block $\tilde{H}_k$, 
which is equivalent to adding it to the full Hessian, i.e.\ $\tilde{H}_i + \delta_w I$,
and the Riccati recursion is repeated with increasing $\delta_w$ until it succeeds.
The trial sequence follows the inertia correction of IPOPT~\cite{wachter2006implementation}
and is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:inertia_correction}, with the IPOPT defaults listed in Table~\ref{tab:inertia_correction}.
Only the primal regularization from IPOPT is retained, as dual regularization is not the primary focus of this work.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\setstretch{1.02}
\caption{\textsf{INERTIA\_LM}, following IPOPT~\cite{wachter2006implementation}}
\label{alg:inertia_correction}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State\textbf{Input:} $\tilde{H}_i$, $\delta_w^{\mathrm{last}}$
\Statex
\State Riccati recursion with $\tilde{H}_i$
\If{succeeds}
    \State $\delta_w^{\mathrm{last}} \gets 0$
    \State \Return
\EndIf
\If{$\delta_w^{\mathrm{last}} = 0$}
    \State $\delta_w \gets \delta_w^0$ 
    \State $\kappa \gets \bar{\kappa}_w^+$
\Else
    \State $\delta_w \gets \max\{\delta_w^{\min}, \kappa_w^- \delta_w^{\mathrm{last}}\}$ 
    \State $\kappa \gets \kappa_w^+$
\EndIf
\While{$\delta_w \le \delta_w^{\max}$}
    \State Riccati recursion with $\tilde{H}_i + \delta_w I$
    \If{succeeds}
        \State $\delta_w^{\mathrm{last}} \gets \delta_w$
        \State \Return
    \EndIf
    \State $\delta_w \gets \kappa\, \delta_w$
\EndWhile
\State \Return failure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

  \caption{Inertia correction parameters.}
  \label{tab:inertia_correction}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{tabular}{ccp{10cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Description} \\
    \midrule
    $\delta_w^0$        & $10^{-4}$  & Initial Hessian perturbation parameter \\
    $\delta_w^{\min}$   & $10^{-16}$ & Minimum allowable regularization parameter \\

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\setstretch{1.02}
\caption{Primal--dual \gls{IPM} implemented in the experiments}
\label{alg:ipm_experiment}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} \gls{OCP}-structured \gls{QP}, primal--dual iterate $z_0$, iteration budget $i_{\mathrm{max}}$
\For{$i = 0$ \textbf{to} $i_{\mathrm{max}}-1$}
    \State compute $r_i^{\mathrm{stat}}, r_i^{\mathrm{eq}}, r_i^{\mathrm{ineq}}, r_i^{\mathrm{comp}}$
    \If{all residuals are below $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ineq}}, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{comp}}$}
        \State \Return current iterate $z_i$
    \EndIf
    \State factorize \gls{LHS} with regularizations \Comment{Proxy units recorded}
    \State Riccati recursion with $r_i^{\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{aff}}$ for $\Delta z^{\mathrm{aff}}$
    \State compute $\mu^{\mathrm{aff}}$ and $\sigma_i \gets (\mu^{\mathrm{aff}}/\mu_i)^3$  \Comment{Mehrotra's probing}
    \State Riccati recursion with $r_i^{\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{pro}}$ for $\Delta z$
    \State compute $\alpha_i^{\mathrm{prim}}, \alpha_i^{\mathrm{dual}}$ by the \gls{FTB}
    \State line search to reduce $\alpha_i^{\mathrm{prim}}$ \Comment{two-type line search}
    \State $z_{i+1}^{\mathrm{prim}} \gets z_i^{\mathrm{prim}} + \alpha_i^{\mathrm{prim}} \Delta z^{\mathrm{prim}}$
    \State $z_{i+1}^{\mathrm{dual}} \gets z_i^{\mathrm{dual}} + \alpha_i^{\mathrm{dual}} \Delta z^{\mathrm{dual}}$
\EndFor
\State \Return Max iteration reached
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Numerical comparison}
\label{sec:comparison}
In this section, we report the numerical results of the ten regularization methods on the nonconvex \glspl{QP} of the benchmark.
The comparison proceeds in three stages, each narrowing the field.
We first compare the six Riccati-based methods against one another, 
in order to identify the strongest representatives of the category. 
These are then set against the full Hessian regularizations, and finally against inertia correction.
Two ablations follow, which replace the Mehrotra's probing with the Mehrotra's conditional predictor--corrector
in Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_mehrotra} and disable the line search in Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_line_search}.

\subsection{Comparison among Riccati-based regularizations}
\label{subsec:comparison_riccati}
We first compare the proposed Riccati-based regularization methods, 
which are the main focus of this work.
As a baseline we also include the unregularized recursion (\textsf{NO\_REGULARIZE}).

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.00\textwidth]{images/experiment/Riccati-based_results/iter_flop_profile_qp_results_riccati.pdf}
   \caption{Iteration profile (left) and proxy unit profile (right)
            for the six Riccati-based methods and the unregularized baseline over the $170$-instance test set.}
    \label{fig:iter-profile-riccati}
\end{figure}

In the left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-riccati}, two trends stand out.
First, each \textsf{PM} dominates its counterpart \textsf{M} once the budget exceeds about $40$ iterations.
Second, among the regularization strategies, \textsf{MIRROR} and \textsf{GMW} are clearly more effective than \textsf{PROJECT}.
These trends validate the insights mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:fail_minimal_perturbation}.
Among all the methods, \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} top the profile.

Besides, it is worth noting that
the unregularized recursion can also solve a non-negligible fraction of the test set, $23$ of the $170$ instances,
which can be explained by the curvature contribution from the barrier term that makes the reduced Hessian positive definite in some instances.

The right panel counts each solved instance at its total factorization cost in proxy units.
The methods based on eigenvalue modification cost more than those based on modified Cholesky factorization as expected,
and the \textsf{PM} methods, which also act on $P_{k+1}$, cost more than their \textsf{M} counterparts.

Taken together, the two panels reveal a robustness ordering,
\{\textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, \textsf{PM\_GMW}\} $>$
\{\textsf{M\_MIRROR}, \textsf{M\_GMW}, \textsf{PM\_PROJECT}\} $>$ \textsf{M\_PROJECT}.
Since \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} solve all instances, 
we therefore focus on the two Riccati-based regularizations in the following comparisons with other regularization categories.

To justify this choice, we rerun \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, \textsf{PM\_GMW}, \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} and \textsf{H\_PROJECT}, 
together with the unregularized baseline, using the conditional Mehrotra's predictor--corrector instead, 
with all other settings unchanged.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{images/experiment/ab_conditional_mehrotra/iteration_qp_results_riccati.pdf}

    of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, \textsf{PM\_GMW}, \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}, \textsf{H\_PROJECT} and the unregularized baseline.}
    \label{fig:iter-profile-corrector}
\end{figure}

Comparing Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-corrector} with the previous results
shows that every method solves fewer instances with the corrector, but the loss is far from uniform.
\textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} drop drastically, 
whereas \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} and \textsf{H\_PROJECT} only drop about $10$ and $5$ cases.
The ranking of Section~\ref{sec:comparison} is thereby reversed, and the two Riccati-based methods sit at the bottom.

But generally speaking,
this supports Mehrotra's probing as the default method for nonconvex \glspl{QP}, as it improves the performance of methods.
Section~\ref{subsec:mehrotra_analysis} discusses why \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} loses far more instances than \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
This section first breaks down the failures in two ways.
Section~\ref{subsec:failure_status} splits them by termination status,
separating numerical breakdowns of the regularized recursion from exhausted iteration budgets.
Section~\ref{subsec:failure_category} splits them by the \gls{OCP} each instance comes from,
which shows what makes instances hard and why some methods perform poorly.
Finally, Section~\ref{subsec:mehrotra_analysis} explains
why the conditional Mehrotra's predictor--corrector affects \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} so differently.

\subsection{Failure by status}
\label{subsec:failure_status}
A run that does not converge ends with one of three exit statuses, shown per method in Figure~\ref{fig:failures-by-status}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Unregularized}: the Cholesky factorization fails \emph{again} after the regularization has been applied, 
    usually caused by numerical indefiniteness.
    \item \emph{Max iterations reached}: the residuals did not fall below their tolerances within the budget $i_\mathrm{max}=120$.
    \item \emph{NaN encountered}: an infinite or undefined value appeared in the residuals, so that the iterate itself has overflowed.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/experiment/discussion/qp_failures_by_status.pdf}
    \caption{Unsolved instances per method on the $170$ instances, excluding \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW},
    stacked by exit status and annotated with the total and its share of the test set.}
    \label{fig:failures-by-status}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:failures-by-status}, 
\textsf{M\_PROJECT} is the only Riccati-based method with numerical breakdowns, 
with $122$ of its $125$ failures stemming from numerical issues.
This confirms the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:fail_minimal_perturbation}, 
where ill-conditioning propagates into numerical indefiniteness or overflow.

Either insight introduced in Section~\ref{sec:fail_minimal_perturbation} resolves these breakdowns.
Abandoning the minimum perturbation rule (\textsf{M\_MIRROR}, \textsf{M\_GMW})
or regularizing the cost-to-go matrices (\textsf{PM\_PROJECT}) leaves only iteration-limit terminations.
Combining both remedies, as in \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW}, achieves the best performance in this benchmark.

Among the full Hessian methods, only \textsf{H\_GMW} still breaks down, on a single instance,
and all other failures reach the iteration limit.
This breakdown comes only from the numerical indefiniteness during the Riccati recursion in the \gls{IPM}.
It remains a potential issue for full Hessian regularization, as rounding errors can still make the Cholesky factorization fail.

\subsection{Failure by category}
\label{subsec:failure_category}
Figure~\ref{fig:failures-by-category} breaks down the same failures by the \gls{OCP} each instance was collected from.
Together with Figure~\ref{fig:indefinite_qp_scatter},
this shows that \textsf{M\_MIRROR} and \textsf{M\_GMW} fail mostly on the wide indefiniteness of \textsl{Dubins car} and \textsl{Robot arm},
whereas the full Hessian methods fail mostly on the narrow indefiniteness of \textsl{Flying robot} and \textsl{Crane}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{images/experiment/discussion/qp_failures_by_category.pdf}
    \caption{Unsolved instances per method and \acrshort{OCP}
    with $n_\mathcal{P}$ as the number of instances collected from that \acrshort{OCP}.}
    \label{fig:failures-by-category}
\end{figure}

For the full Hessian regularizations and \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}, \textsl{Flying robot} is the hardest \gls{OCP},
and, as Figure~\ref{fig:indefinite_source_signature} shows, its indefiniteness lies only in $P_k$ and at few stages.
\textsf{H\_PROJECT}, \textsf{H\_MIRROR} and \textsf{H\_GMW} fail on $19$, $20$ and $20$ of its $20$ instances,
and \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} on $8$, which are $8$ of its $9$ failures,
while every Riccati-based method except \textsf{M\_PROJECT} solves all of them.

Since the full Hessian regularizations and \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} cannot directly act on $P_k$, 
they perturb the $\tilde{H}_k$ over the whole horizon. 
This slows down the convergence and thus exhausts the iteration budget.
The Riccati-based regularization avoids this by treating an indefinite stage where it arises in the recursion
and only affecting the stages preceding it.

\subsection{Analysis of the Mehrotra ablation}
\label{subsec:mehrotra_analysis}
Based on the ablation in Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_mehrotra},
this subsection explains why the ranking of Section~\ref{sec:comparison} reverses 
under the conditional Mehrotra's predictor--corrector,
i.e.\ why \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} loses far more instances than \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.

With the simplified notation of \eqref{eq:schur_comp_KKT_reduced},
the regularized system and the resulting bound on the direction read
\begin{equation}
    (\tilde{M}_i + E_i)\, \Delta y_z = -r_i,
    \qquad
    \|\Delta y_z\| \le \frac{\|r_i\|}{\nu_{\min}(\tilde{M}_i+E_i)}.
    \label{eq:step_bound}
\end{equation}
The bound tightens as $E_i$ raises $\nu_{\min}(\tilde{M}_i+E_i)$, 
and the two methods raise it by different amounts.
\textsf{INERTIA\_LM} increases a uniform shift $\delta_w$ until the factorization succeeds,
commonly beyond the smallest successful shift $\delta_w^\ast$,
so $\nu_{\min}(\tilde{M}_i+E_i) \ge \delta_w-\delta_w^\ast$ stays of the order of $\delta_w$.
\textsf{PM\_MIRROR} modifies only the negative eigenvalues of $\tilde{M}_k$ and $P_k$,
which allows $\nu_{\min}(\tilde{M}_i+E_i)$ to stay as a small positive value.
\textsf{PM\_MIRROR} could thus reach a much longer direction than \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} at the same $\|r_i\|$.
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:step_metrics}, the median affine direction of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} is longer than that of \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Regularized step metrics of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.}
\label{tab:step_metrics}
\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} & \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{l}{\emph{Corrector, on $170$ instances}} \\
\quad Median affine direction $\|\Delta y_z\|$             & $88.7$ & $5.6$ \\
\quad Percentage of rejected corrector steps          & 4.5\,\% & 55.7\,\% \\
\addlinespace
\multicolumn{3}{l}{\emph{Probing, on $161$ instances solved by both}} \\
\quad Total iterations                                      & $4056$ & $5261$ \\
\quad Regularized iterations                          & $1572$ & $2808$ \\
\quad Median displacement $\alpha^{\mathrm{prim}}\|\Delta y_z\|$ & $1.61$ & $0.38$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

In \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, the long affine direction makes the second-order term dominate the complementarity residual in the \gls{RHS},
so the corrector direction is long as well.
The \gls{FTB} rule then allows only short step sizes for both, which keep $\mu^{\mathrm{aff}}$ and $\mu^{\mathrm{cor}}$ close to $\mu$,
so the condition $\mu^{\mathrm{cor}} \le 2\mu^{\mathrm{aff}}$ rarely rejects the corrector steps.
The corrector step, dominated by the inexactness of the affine direction, is thus taken,
which cannot efficiently reduce the complementarity residual or the duality measure.
In \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}, by contrast, the second-order term usually stays comparable to the rest of the complementarity residual,
so the condition can still reject the corrector steps as shown in Table~\ref{tab:step_metrics} and the scheme makes progress as intended.
\textsf{INERTIA\_LM} thus loses far fewer instances than \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}.

With probing, the affine direction only affects the centering parameter,
so the inexactness no longer enters the search direction.
The longer direction of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, a drawback with the corrector, thus becomes an advantage,
as measured by the displacement $\alpha^{\mathrm{prim}}\|\Delta y_z\|$ in Table~\ref{tab:step_metrics}.
\textsf{INERTIA\_LM} moves less per regularized iteration and generally needs more regularized iterations,
while \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} moves further per regularized iteration.
This larger displacement could help the iterate move towards the inequality constraint boundaries,
where the curvature contribution could make the reduced Hessian positive definite and thus restore fast convergence.
