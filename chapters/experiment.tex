\chapter{Numerical experiments} % Overview text
\label{Chapter::Experiments}
In this chapter, we evaluate the proposed regularization methods numerically and discuss the results.
We begin in Section~\ref{sec:metrics} with the two metrics used throughout the comparison, 
the iteration profile and the proxy unit estimation, which measure convergence behavior and factorization cost respectively.
Section~\ref{sec:prototype} then describes the prototype solver on which all experiments are run 
and stresses that it reproduces the iteration behavior of HPIPM on convex \glspl{QP}, 
so that any difference observed later can be attributed to the regularization rather than to the solver itself.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} fixes the experiment setup, 
naming and classifying the regularization methods under comparison and stating the parameters shared by all of them.
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} presents the numerical comparison, 
first among the Riccati-based methods themselves and then against full Hessian regularization and inertia correction,
followed by ablations of the barrier update and the line search.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} concludes the chapter by analyzing the failures and the Mehrotra ablation.

\section{Experiment metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
In this section, we will introduce the metrics used to evaluate the performance of different regularization strategies in our experiments.
Since we care about both the convergence behavior and the factorization cost caused by different regularization methods, 
we will use two complementary metrics: iteration profile and proxy unit estimation.
The former, iteration profile, is used to evaluate how many iterations each method takes to converge on a set of test instances, 
while the latter, flop unit estimation, is approximated by the modified Cholesky factorizations of the reduced Hessian
and is used to evaluate the factorization cost of each method.

\subsection{Iteration profile}
The \emph{iteration profile} shows the number of test instances 
that the method solves within certain iteration budget.
An instance where the method fails to solve within the max iteration budget $i_\mathrm{max}$ counts as unsolved.

We plot the iteration profile as a curve, 
with the iteration budget from $1$ to $i_\mathrm{max}$ on the horizontal axis 
and the number of solved instances on the vertical axis.
Different from the common performance profile~\cite{dolanBenchmarkingOptimizationSoftware2002},
which uses the iteration ratio to the best method as horizontal axis,
the iteration profile of a method does not depend on the other methods,
by which we emphasize the ability of each method itself to solve the instances.

Plotting the profiles of all methods in one figure allows a direct comparison:
at any budget, the higher curve belongs to the method that has solved more instances.
And a curve that rises steeply at small budgets indicates an \emph{efficient} method,
and its final value at $i_\mathrm{max}$, the number of solved instances, measures its \emph{robustness}.

\subsection{Flop unit estimation}
The regularization strategies differ in the factorization they perform per iteration.
Some use an eigenvalue decomposition, some a modified Cholesky factorization,
and some repeat the Cholesky factorization with an increasing diagonal shift.
Iteration counts alone therefore do not reflect the factorization cost,
so we express this cost in a common proxy unit.

We take one Cholesky factorization of the block reduced Hessian as the proxy unit. 
For factorizing an $n_u \times n_u$ matrix, the cost is approximately $\tfrac{1}{3} n_u^3$ flops, hence
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{unit} \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{3}\, n_u^3 .
\end{equation}
The modified factorization only adds diagonal updates of $\mathcal{O}(n_u^2)$ cost, 
so to leading order it also costs one unit.

The other factorizations and decompositions are expressed relative to this unit.
A symmetric eigenvalue decomposition of the same block costs 
about $9 n_u^3$ flops \cite{verschuerenTimeoptimalRaceCar2016b}, i.e.
\begin{equation}
\frac{9 n_u^3}{\tfrac{1}{3} n_u^3} \;=\; 27 \ \text{units}.
\end{equation}

Regarding the cost-to-go matrix with dimension $n_x \times n_x$ 
and a full Hessian block with dimension ${(n_x+n_u) \times (n_x+n_u)}$, 
the ratios are considered as:
\begin{equation}
\omega_P \;=\; \left(\frac{n_x}{n_u}\right)^{3}, \quad
\omega_H \;=\; \left(\frac{n_x+n_u}{n_u}\right)^{3}.
\end{equation}

Thus, a modified Cholesky factorization of cost-to-go matrix costs $\omega_P$ units and an eigenvalue decomposition $27\omega_P$ units.

Likewise, the two factorizations of a full Hessian block cost $\omega_H$ and $27\omega_H$ units.
Since the full Hessian regularization modifies all $N$ blocks $\tilde{H}_k$ at once,
each regularization costs $N\omega_H$ or $27N\omega_H$ units.

The factorization cost of a run is thus the sum of two parts,
the Cholesky factorizations of the Riccati recursion itself and
the additional factorizations introduced by the regularization.
The latter includes every rerun of the Riccati recursion, whose factorizations are counted again,
and every failed Cholesky factorization, which counts as one proxy unit, although it may stop early.

\section{Prototype solver implementation}
\label{sec:prototype}
To ensure a fair comparison, we implemented all regularization methods in one prototype solver.
The Riccati-based primal--dual \gls{IPM} prototype solver is implemented in Python, using HPIPM as a reference.
The prototype was designed to reproduce its iteration behavior on convex \glspl{QP} and is available at \url{https://github.com/Pandatheon/Primal-dual_IPM.git}.

One implementation detail deserves mention, 
as it deviates from the plain \gls{FTB} rule stated in Section~\ref{sec:size_control}. 
Rather than being a fixed fraction $\eta$ of the maximal step, 
the primal and dual step sizes $\alpha_{\mathrm{prim}}$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{dual}}$ are obtained 
from their respective maximal steps through the heuristic
\begin{equation}
  \alpha =
  \begin{cases}
    \alpha_{\mathrm{max}}\big[(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{max}})\,\kappa_\eta + \alpha_{\mathrm{max}}\hat{\eta}\big], & \alpha_{\mathrm{max}} < 1,\\
    \alpha_{\mathrm{max}}, & \alpha_{\mathrm{max}} = 1,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{\mathrm{max}}\in(0,1]$ denotes the maximal step length from Equation~\eqref{eq:ftb_max_prim} or Equation~\eqref{eq:ftb_max_dual},
$\kappa_\eta = 0.99$ and $\hat{\eta} = 1-10^{-7}$.

When $\alpha_{\mathrm{max}} < 1$, 
the bracketed factor changes with $\alpha_{\mathrm{max}}$, 
from a strongly blocked step to a nearly full one, so the closer the maximal step is to a full step, the less it is shortened.
When $\alpha_{\mathrm{max}} = 1$,
an unblocked full step can be taken without scaling by $\hat{\eta}$ so that
the local quadratic convergence rate of the \gls{IPM} can be preserved.

We further align the prototype solver with HPIPM in terms of the mode settings by defining a set of HPIPM settings, 
referred to in the following as the \texttt{CUSTOMIZED} mode, whose values are listed in Table~\ref{tab:customized_settings}.

As one can notice, the \texttt{CUSTOMIZED} mode largely coincides with the \texttt{SPEED} mode of the original HPIPM, 
differing in three respects. 
First, the square-root algorithm is disabled (\texttt{square\_root\_alg}$=0$)
so that the Riccati recursion requires no \gls{PD} full Hessian to carry on,
second, the primal and dual step sizes are computed separately (\texttt{split\_step}$=1$) instead of sharing a single step length,
and third, the minimal step size is disabled by setting \texttt{alpha\_min}$=0$.
In both modes no iterative refinement is applied (\texttt{itref\_pred\_max}$=$\texttt{itref\_corr\_max}$=0$) 
and the conditional Mehrotra's predictor--corrector is retained (\texttt{pred\_corr}$=$\texttt{cond\_pred\_corr}$=1$).

To verify that the two implementations do agree, we collected $40$ convex \gls{QP} subproblems, 
five from each of the $8$ \glspl{OCP} in the benchmark, 
and compared the iteration behavior of the prototype solver against that of \texttt{CUSTOMIZED}-mode HPIPM. 
The resulting iteration profiles are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:convex_iteration_profile}. 

Both solvers solve all $40$ instances in the exact same number of iterations for each instance, 
resulting in iteration profiles that coincide.
On convex \glspl{QP}, the prototype solver therefore reproduces the iteration behavior of HPIPM, 
which verifies the prototype as a reliable baseline and allows us to attribute the differences observed in the experiments to the regularization 
rather than to the solver.

\section{Experiment setup}
\label{sec:setup}
In this section, 
the settings for the experiment are introduced.
First, based on the previously discussed methods, 
we will name and classify the regularization methods considered in the experiment, 
and then we will introduce the general setup of the experiments, 
including the convergence criterion and step size control strategy.

\subsection{Evaluated regularizations}
\label{subsec:experimented_regularizations}
Before turning to the results, we fix the set of regularization methods entering the comparison, 
together with the naming convention used throughout this chapter.

Every method is named in the form \textsf{TARGET\_STRATEGY}.
The \textsf{TARGET} is the matrix from which the correction is derived and to which it is applied,
which gives the three categories of \emph{full Hessian regularization}, \emph{Riccati-based regularization}, and \emph{inertia correction}.
The \textsf{STRATEGY} is one of the numerical strategies of Section~\ref{subsec:reg_strategies},
which are largely independent of the matrix they act on and can therefore be paired with each target.

\paragraph{Full Hessian regularization}
The first category follows Section~\ref{subsec:full_hessian} and takes the full Hessian as the target matrix.
The regularization is triggered by the failure of the Cholesky factorization in the Riccati recursion, 
and is applied stage-wise to each full Hessian block.
The Riccati recursion is then re-attempted with the regularized full Hessian.
Applying the three strategies of Section~\ref{subsec:reg_strategies} to each block $\tilde{H}_k$
gives \textsf{H\_PROJECT}, \textsf{H\_MIRROR} and \textsf{H\_GMW}.

\paragraph{Riccati-based regularization}
The second category is the one proposed in Section~\ref{sec:riccati_primal_reg} and acts on the reduced Hessian $\tilde{M}$.
The algorithms are given in Algorithms~\ref{alg:modified_riccati_recursion} and \ref{alg:improved_modified_riccati_recursion},
which differ in which matrices of the backward pass the adjustment is derived from and applied to, 
giving two targets within this category:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textsf{M}: only the block reduced Hessian $\tilde{M}_k$ is corrected, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:modified_riccati_recursion}. 
  The correction is purely local and serves as a minimal safeguard for the factorization.
  \item \textsf{PM}: the cost-to-go matrix $P_{k+1}$ entering the stage is corrected as well, 
  as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:improved_modified_riccati_recursion} introduced in this work, 
  so that the analyzed propagation of negative curvature is taken into account.
\end{itemize}

Pairing each target with the three strategies gives six methods,
which test the two insights of Section~\ref{sec:fail_minimal_perturbation},
abandoning the minimum perturbation rule and correcting the cost-to-go matrix, separately and in combination.

\textsf{M\_PROJECT} applies neither and serves as the reference, 
\textsf{M\_MIRROR} and \textsf{M\_GMW} apply only the first,
\textsf{PM\_PROJECT} only the second, and \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} apply both.

\paragraph{Inertia correction}
The third category is the \gls{LM}-style inertia correction 
widely used in IPOPT~\cite{wachter2006implementation}, Fatrop~\cite{vanroye2023fatrop} and DDP~\cite{tassa2012synthesis},
which we denote by \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.

In this category, a scalar multiple of the identity $\delta_w I$ is added to each full Hessian block $\tilde{H}_k$, 
i.e.\ $\tilde{H}_k + \delta_w I$, and the Riccati recursion is repeated with increasing $\delta_w$ until it succeeds.

The trial sequence follows IPOPT:
the unmodified matrix is tried first,
and then $\delta_w$ starts from $\delta_w^0$, or from the last successful value reduced by $\kappa_w^-$,
and is increased by $\kappa_w^+$, until the Riccati recursion succeeds or $\delta_w$ exceeds $\delta_w^{\max}$.
The parameters are the IPOPT defaults listed in Table~\ref{tab:inertia_correction}.
Only the primal regularization from IPOPT is retained, as dual regularization is not the primary focus of this work.
\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Inertia correction parameters.}
  \label{tab:inertia_correction}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{tabular}{ccp{10cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Description} \\
    \midrule
    $\delta_w^0$        & $10^{-4}$  & Initial Hessian perturbation parameter \\
    $\delta_w^{\min}$   & $10^{-16}$ & Minimum allowable regularization parameter \\
    $\delta_w^{\max}$   & $10^{16}$  & Maximum allowable regularization threshold \\
    $\kappa_w^-$        & $1/3$      & Factor to decrease perturbation upon success \\
    $\kappa_w^+$        & $8$        & Factor to increase perturbation after inertia failure \\
    $\bar{\kappa}_w^+$  & $10^2$     & Accelerated scaling factor for severe indefiniteness \\
    $\delta_{\text{LM}}$& $0.0$      & Initial \gls{LM} shift (unmodified matrix attempted first) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

This method sits between the two preceding categories, as it has no distinct target matrix in the above sense.
Since $\delta_w I$ is a uniform shift along every direction, applying it to $\tilde{Q}_k$ and $\tilde{R}_k$ in the full Hessian blocks $\tilde{H}_k$
adds the same term to the reduced Hessian blocks $\tilde{M}_k$ and $P_k$ in the Riccati recursion.

\section{Numerical comparison}
\label{sec:comparison}
In this section, we report the numerical results of the ten regularization methods on the nonconvex \glspl{QP} of the benchmark.
The comparison proceeds in three stages, each narrowing the field.
We first compare the six Riccati-based methods against one another, 
in order to identify the strongest representatives of the category. 
These are then set against the full Hessian regularizations, and finally against inertia correction.

\subsection{Comparison among Riccati-based regularizations}
We first compare the proposed Riccati-based regularization methods, 
which are the main focus of this work.
As a baseline we also include the unregularized recursion (\textsf{NO\_REGULARIZE}). 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/experiment/Riccati-based_results/iteration_qp_results_riccati.pdf}
    \caption{Iteration profile of the six Riccati-based methods over the $175$-instance test set:
    number of instances solved within a given iteration budget.}
    \label{fig:iter-profile-riccati}
\end{figure}

In the iteration profile of Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-riccati}, two trends stand out.
First, each \textsf{PM} dominates its counterpart \textsf{M} once the budget exceeds about $40$ iterations.
Second, among the regularization strategies, \textsf{MIRROR} and \textsf{GMW} are clearly more effective than \textsf{PROJECT}.
These trends validate the insights mentioned in Section \ref{sec:fail_minimal_perturbation}.
Among all the methods, \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} tops the profile with \textsf{PM\_GMW} close behind.

Besides, it is worth noting that
the unregularized recursion can also solve a non-negligible fraction of the test set, around $30$ of the $175$ instances,
which can be explained by the curvature contribution from the barrier term that makes the reduced Hessian \gls{PD} in some instances.

Figure~\ref{fig:flop-profile-riccati} shows the distribution of the factorization cost per instance in proxy units for each method.
Instances that reach the iteration cap $i_\mathrm{max}$ are included with the cost of all $i_\mathrm{max}$ iterations,
so the distribution reflects the worst-case cost.

As we can notice, 
all methods share the same lowest cost, since some instances are solved by the unregularized recursion.
Beyond that, as expected,
the \gls{EM}-based methods cost more than the \gls{MC}-based ones,
and the \textsf{PM} methods, which also act on $P_{k+1}$, cost more than their \textsf{M} counterparts.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/experiment/Riccati-based_results/decomp_flop_worstcase_qp_results_riccati.pdf}
    \caption{Worst-case factorization cost of the six Riccati-based methods (proxy units, log scale).
    Annotations report the maximum observed cost and the number of unsolved instances.}
    \label{fig:flop-profile-riccati}
\end{figure}

Taken together, the two figures first reveal a robustness ordering,
\textsf{PM\_MIRROR} $>$ \textsf{PM\_GMW} $>$
\{\textsf{M\_MIRROR}, \textsf{M\_GMW}, \textsf{PM\_PROJECT}\} $>$ \textsf{M\_PROJECT}.
If reliability is the priority, \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} is the safest choice, leaving the fewest instances unsolved.
If a balance between reliability and factorization cost is preferred, \textsf{PM\_GMW} is the more attractive option.
We will therefore focus on \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} in the following comparisons with other regularization categories.

\subsection{Comparison with full Hessian regularizations}
Having identified \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} as the strongest Riccati-based methods, 
we now compare them against the full Hessian regularizations.

The iteration profile in Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-fullhessian} 
shows that the two Riccati-based methods sit well above every full Hessian variant across the whole iteration budget,
which indicating better performance in efficiency and robustness of two Riccati-based methods over full Hessian regularization.

In Figure~\ref{fig:flop-profile-fullhessian}, 
the two \textsf{PM} methods also outperform on the factorization cost.
The worst-case costs of \textsf{H\_PROJECT} and \textsf{H\_MIRROR} lie far above those of both Riccati-based methods,

The Riccati-based methods are thus generally more robust and cheaper than every full Hessian counterpart.
This supports regularizing the reduced Hessian by exploiting the stage-wise structure of the problem rather than the full Hessian.

In Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-inertia}, the three regularized methods end close together, indicating similar robustness of three methods.
However, beyond about $30$ iterations the \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} sit clearly above \textsf{INERTIA\_LM},

From the evidence of this benchmark,

\subsection{Ablation on the conditional Mehrotra}
\label{subsec:ablation_mehrotra}
All results so far use Mehrotra's probing.
To justify this choice, we rerun \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, \textsf{PM\_GMW}, \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} and \textsf{H\_PROJECT}, 
together with the unregularized baseline, using the conditional Mehrotra's predictor--corrector instead, with all other settings unchanged.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/experiment/ab_conditional_mehrotra/iteration_qp_results_riccati.pdf}
    \caption{Iteration profile with the conditional predictor--corrector
    of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, \textsf{PM\_GMW}, \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}, \textsf{H\_PROJECT} and the unregularized baseline.}
    \label{fig:iter-profile-corrector}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-corrector} shows that every method solves fewer instances with the corrector, but the loss is far from uniform.
\textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW} drop drastically, whereas \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} and \textsf{H\_PROJECT} only drop about $10$ cases.
The ranking of Section~\ref{sec:comparison} is thereby reversed, and the two Riccati-based methods sit at the bottom.

But generally speaking,
this supports Mehrotra's probing as the default method for nonconvex \glspl{QP}, as it improves the performance of methods generally.
Section~\ref{subsec:mehrotra_analysis} discusses why \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} loses far more instances than \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.

\subsection{Ablation on the line search}
\label{subsec:ablation_line_search}
The two-phase line search of Section~\ref{subsec:two_phase_search} was introduced for 
investigating the effect of line search on nonconvex \glspl{QP}.
To isolate its contribution, we disable it, so that the \gls{FTB} step is taken directly,
and rerun the same five methods as in the previous subsection with all other settings unchanged.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/experiment/ab_line_search_results/iteration_qp_results_riccati.pdf}
    \caption{Iteration profile without the line search
    of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, \textsf{PM\_GMW}, \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}, \textsf{H\_PROJECT} and the unregularized baseline.}
    \label{fig:iter-profile-nols}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:iter-profile-nols} shows that the line search matters much less than the barrier update.
Only \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} clearly benefits from the line search, while the other methods solve almost the same number of instances,
and the similar curves indicate that most iteration counts are identical on the instances solved in both settings.

The ranking of Section~\ref{sec:comparison} therefore does not rely on the line search.
The robustness and efficiency of the regularization method stem from itself.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
This section first breaks down the failures in two ways.
Section~\ref{subsec:failure_status} splits them by termination status,
separating numerical breakdowns of the regularized recursion from exhausted iteration budgets.
Section~\ref{subsec:failure_category} splits them by the \gls{OCP} each instance comes from,
which shows what makes instances hard and why some methods perform poorly.
Finally, Section~\ref{subsec:mehrotra_analysis} explains
why the conditional Mehrotra's predictor--corrector affects \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} so differently.

\subsection{Failure by status}
\label{subsec:failure_status}
A run that does not converge ends with one of three exit statuses, shown per method in Figure~\ref{fig:failures-by-status}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Unregularized}: the Cholesky factorization fails \emph{again} after the regularization has been applied, 
    usually caused by numerical indefiniteness.
    \item \emph{Max iterations reached}: the residuals did not fall below their tolerances within the budget $i_\mathrm{max}=120$.
    \item \emph{NaN encountered}: an infinite or undefined value appeared in the residuals, so that the iterate itself has overflowed.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{images/experiment/discussion/qp_failures_by_status.pdf}
    \caption{Unsolved instances per method on the $175$-instance test set, 
    stacked by exit status and annotated with the total and its share of the test set.}
    \label{fig:failures-by-status}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:failures-by-status}, 
\textsf{M\_PROJECT} is the only Riccati-based method with numerical breakdowns, with $110$ of its $113$ failures stemming from numerical issues.
This confirms the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:fail_minimal_perturbation}, 
where ill-conditioning propagates into numerical indefiniteness or overflow.

Either remedy identified there resolves these breakdowns.
Abandoning the minimum perturbation rule (\textsf{M\_MIRROR}, \textsf{M\_GMW})
or adding the cost-to-go correction (\textsf{PM\_PROJECT}) leaves only iteration-limit terminations.
Combining both remedies, as in \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{PM\_GMW}, achieves the best performance in this benchmark.

The full Hessian methods still break down on $4$, $5$ and $6$ instances,
but most of their failures reach the iteration limit.
But their breakdowns come from only the numerical indefiniteness that arises in the \gls{IPM}.

In the nonconvex case, local minimizers frequently lie on the constraint boundary. 
Near convergence, the barrier term $\Lambda_k T_k^{-1}$ grows large as the slacks of active constraints approach zero, 
and enters $\tilde{M}_k$ both directly through $\tilde{R}_k$ and recursively through $P_{k+1}$.
Once $\kappa_2(\tilde{M}_k)\,\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} \ge 1$, where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$ denotes the machine precision, 
rounding errors exceed the smallest eigenvalue of $\tilde{M}_k$, causing its Cholesky factorization to fail.

For the full Hessian methods, although every $\tilde{H}_k^\mathrm{reg}$ is already \gls{PD},
the Cholesky factorization of $\tilde{M}_{k-1}$ could still fail due to rounding errors.
The Riccati-based methods, however, correct $\tilde{M}_{k-1}$ itself whenever its factorization fails,
so the indefiniteness is handled whether it stems from the problem or from rounding.

\subsection{Failure by category}
\label{subsec:failure_category}
Figure~\ref{fig:failures-by-category} breaks down the same failures by the \gls{OCP} each instance was collected from.
Combined with Figure~\ref{fig:indefinite_qp_scatter}, 
it suggests that an instance becomes hard only when its indefiniteness is both deep and wide.
Deep but narrow indefiniteness, as in \textsl{Crane} and \textsl{Flying robot}, and wide but moderate indefiniteness, as in \textsl{Dubins car} and \textsl{Robot arm},
both remain solvable, as \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} solves every instance of these four \glspl{OCP}.
\textsl{Acrobot} is the only \gls{OCP} that combines both, and its four unsolved instances defeat every method within the iteration budget.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{images/experiment/discussion/qp_failures_by_category.pdf}
    \caption{Unsolved instances per method and \acrshort{OCP};
    $n_\mathcal{P}$ is the number of instances collected from that \acrshort{OCP}.}
    \label{fig:failures-by-category}
\end{figure}

For the full Hessian methods, \textsl{Flying robot} is the hardest \gls{OCP},
and, as Figure~\ref{fig:indefinite_source_signature} shows, its indefiniteness lies only in $P_k$ and at few stages.
\textsf{H\_PROJECT}, \textsf{H\_MIRROR} and \textsf{H\_GMW} fail on $19$, $20$ and $20$ of its $20$ instances,
while every Riccati-based method except \textsf{M\_PROJECT} solves all of them.

Since the full Hessian methods cannot directly act on $P_k$, 
they perturb the $\tilde{H}_k$ over the whole horizon, including the $\tilde{Q}_k$, $\tilde{R}_k$ and $\tilde{S}_k$.
This slows down the convergence and thus exhaust the iteration budget.
The Riccati-based regularization avoids this by treating indefiniteness where it arises in the recursion.

\subsection{Analysis of the Mehrotra ablation}
\label{subsec:mehrotra_analysis}
Based on the ablation in Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_mehrotra},
this subsection explains why the ranking of Section~\ref{sec:comparison} reverses under the conditional Mehrotra corrector,
i.e.\ why \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} loses far more instances than \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.

With the simplified notation of Equation~\eqref{eq:schur_comp_KKT_reduced},
the regularized system and its step bound read
\begin{equation}
    (\tilde{M}_i + E_i)\, \Delta y_z = -r_i,
    \qquad
    \|\Delta y_z\| \le \frac{\|r_i\|}{\nu_{\min}(\tilde{M}_i+E_i)}.
    \label{eq:step_bound}
\end{equation}
As one can notice, the regularization would shorten the step taken,
and the methods differ in how small $\nu_{\min}(\tilde{M}_i+E_i)$ can become,
and thus in how long the regularized step is. 

\textsf{INERTIA\_LM} increases a uniform shift $\delta$ until the factorization succeeds. 
If $\delta_{\min}$ is the smallest shift for which it succeeds, then $\nu_{\min}(\tilde{M}_i+E_i) \ge \delta-\delta_{\min}$.
The accepted $\delta$ usually is larger than $\delta_{\min}$, and $\nu_{\min}$ stays of the order of $\delta$.

\textsf{PM\_MIRROR} modifies only the negative eigenvalues of $\tilde{M}_k$ and $P_k$,
which allows $\nu_{\min}(\tilde{M}_i+E_i)$ to stay as small positive value.
\textsf{PM\_MIRROR} could thus reach a much longer step than \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} at the same $\|r_i\|$.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:step_metrics}, the median affine step of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} is longer than that of \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.
This difference can be amplified in Mehrotra's predictor--corrector scheme, 
where the second-order term $\Delta T_{\mathrm{aff}}\Delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{aff}}\mathbf{1}$ grows quadratically with the affine step.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Step metrics of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} and \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}.}
\label{tab:step_metrics}
\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} & \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{l}{\emph{Corrector, on $175$ instances}} \\
\quad Median affine step $\|\Delta y_z\|$             & $65.4$ & $4.4$ \\
\quad Percentage of rejected corrector steps          & 3.7\,\% & 39.1\,\% \\
\addlinespace
\multicolumn{3}{l}{\emph{Probing, on $164$ instances solved by both}} \\
\quad Iterations                                      & $4218$ & $5132$ \\
\quad Regularized iterations                          & $1566$ & $2289$ \\
\quad Median displacement $\alpha_{\mathrm{prim}}\|\Delta y_z\|$ & $1.44$ & $0.47$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

In \textsf{PM\_MIRROR},
the long affine step makes the second-order term dominate the \gls{RHS}, so the corrector step is long as well.
For both steps, the \gls{FTB} rule then allows only a short step size,
which keeps $\mu_{\mathrm{aff}}$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{cor}}$ close to $\mu$.
The condition $\mu_{\mathrm{cor}} < 2\mu_{\mathrm{aff}}$ thus rarely detects this.
The corrector step dominated by the inexactness from an affine step is, therefore, taken,
which can not efficiently reduce the complementarity residual or the duality gap.

In \textsf{INERTIA\_LM}, however, the second-order term is usually still comparable in size to the rest of the \gls{RHS},
so the conditional Mehrotra's predictor--corrector scheme remains effective,
and the condition can still reject the corrector steps as shown in Table~\ref{tab:step_metrics}.
The scheme thus makes progress as intended, and \textsf{INERTIA\_LM} loses far fewer instances than \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}.

With probing, the affine step only affects the centering parameter,
so the inexactness no longer enters the search direction.
The longer step of \textsf{PM\_MIRROR}, which is a drawback under the corrector, thus becomes an advantage,
as measured by the displacement $\alpha_{\mathrm{prim}}\|\Delta y_z\|$ in Table~\ref{tab:step_metrics}.

\textsf{INERTIA\_LM} moves less per regularized iteration and generally needs more regularized iterations,
while \textsf{PM\_MIRROR} moves further per regularized iteration.
This larger displacement could help the iterate to move towards the constraint boundaries,
where the reduced Hessian could be \gls{PD} with the curvature contribution, and recover the fast convergence towards solutions.
